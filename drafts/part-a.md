# Part A: Dabur India

*All figures consolidated, ₹ crore, years ending 31 March. Sources and definitions for every number are in the numbers table.*

**Definitions used throughout (and for Marico in Part B).** Revenue = revenue from operations. Operating profit (EBIT) = profit before exceptional items and tax, minus other income, plus finance costs. Net profit for margins and ROE = profit attributable to owners; equity = closing equity attributable to owners. Borrowings = current plus non-current borrowings, excluding lease liabilities. Capital employed = total equity including minority interest, plus borrowings. Cost of goods sold is built as materials consumed + purchases of stock-in-trade + change in inventories, because the statutory P&L has no COGS line. All balance-sheet ratios use closing balances.

## 1. What it sells, and to whom

Dabur makes everyday herbal and Ayurvedic products: hair oils, toothpaste, Chyawanprash, honey, digestives, home care, juices and, since the Badshah acquisition, spices. It sells them through distributors and retailers to Indian households, and also sells in the Middle East, Africa, Bangladesh, Turkey and the US. The full-year India and overseas split is not in the audited statements, whose segment note is by product; the Q4 FY26 investor presentation puts overseas at ₹834 cr, about 27% of that quarter's revenue. The largest and fastest-growing reportable segment is **Consumer Care, ₹10,864 cr in FY26, up 6.9%** from ₹10,160 cr (82% of revenue). Food fell 4.0% to ₹2,016 cr; Retail, other segments and unallocated make up the remaining ₹313 cr (FY26 and FY25 segment notes).

## 2. How it makes money on each unit

The unit is one pack sold into the trade. Dabur does not disclose revenue or cost per pack; I looked in the FY26 audited results, the segment note and the Q4 FY26 investor presentation. So I work with ₹100 of revenue:

| Out of every ₹100 of FY26 revenue | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Cost of goods (materials, traded goods, inventory change) | 51.7 |
| **Left after goods (gross margin)** | **48.3** |
| Advertising | 6.7 |
| Employees | 10.4 |
| Other expenses | 12.5 |
| Depreciation | 3.6 |
| **Operating profit (EBIT)** | **15.0** |

The cost choice that explains the margin: **Dabur makes most of what it sells, across hundreds of products, in its own plants.** That keeps the gross margin high (48.3% against Marico's 44.5%) but it carries a heavy fixed layer below it: employees at 10.4% of revenue and depreciation at 3.6%, against 6.7% and 1.5% at Marico. More than half of the gross margin goes on employees, other expenses and depreciation. I would expect much of that to fall more slowly than volume if sales dipped, though the statements do not split fixed from variable costs. That is why a strong gross margin becomes only a 15% operating margin.

## 3. Whether its profit becomes cash

| | FY24 | FY25 | FY26 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profit for the year (group) | 1,811 | 1,740 | 1,869 |
| Cash from operations | 2,013 | 1,987 | 2,579 |
| **Gap (cash − profit)** | **+202** | **+246** | **+710** |

Cash has been above profit every year, 121% of profit over three years. The biggest reconciling number is depreciation (₹469 cr in FY26), but it is mostly cancelled by interest income on Dabur's investments, which is taken out of operating cash and shown under investing (−₹366 cr in FY26). The line that actually explains the gap is **trade payables: suppliers funded ₹752 cr over three years** (+₹217 cr, +₹407 cr, +₹128 cr). In FY26 the jump to +₹710 cr also came from **receivables falling by ₹177 cr** (₹889 cr to ₹715 cr, receivable days 25.8 to 19.8) and taxes paid being ₹153 cr below the tax charge. The annual results do not say why receivables fell; it could be faster collection or a shift to channels that pay sooner. This is cash above profit because suppliers wait, not only because of non-cash charges, so I would want to know whether payable terms can stretch further.

What Dabur did with ₹6,579 cr of operating cash over three years: paid **₹3,360 cr in dividends** (51%), spent **₹1,554 cr on capex** (24%), and put the rest into investments, ₹1,185 cr net in FY26 alone. Borrowings ended the period lower (₹1,158 cr to ₹1,063 cr).

## 4. What its ratios say, and where they mislead

| | FY24 | FY25 | FY26 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue (₹ cr) | 12,404 | 12,563 | 13,193 |
| Operating margin | 16.1% | 14.9% | 15.0% |
| Net margin | 14.9% | 14.1% | 14.4% |
| ROCE: (PBT + finance costs) ÷ capital employed | 21.7% | 20.3% | 20.0% |
| ROE: owners' profit ÷ closing owners' equity | 18.7% | 16.4% | 16.6% |

**DuPont, FY26, closing balances:**
ROE = (1,895 ÷ 13,193) × (13,193 ÷ 17,480) × (17,480 ÷ 11,420) = 0.1436 × 0.7547 × 1.5307 = **16.6%**, which equals 1,895 ÷ 11,420. In FY24 the same three factors were 0.1486 × 0.8202 × 1.5328 = 18.7%. The fall in ROE came from margin and turnover, not leverage.

**Cash conversion cycle (closing balances; inventory and payables on built COGS):**

| Days | FY25 | FY26 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Receivables (₹889 cr → ₹715 cr) | 25.81 | 19.79 | −6.02 |
| Inventory (₹2,300 cr → ₹2,322 cr) | 128.47 | 124.18 | −4.29 |
| Payables (₹2,825 cr → ₹2,954 cr) | 157.81 | 158.03 | +0.22 |
| **Cycle** | **−3.53** | **−14.06** | **−10.53** |

−6.02 − 4.29 − 0.22 = −10.53. A negative cycle means suppliers fund Dabur's stock and credit, and receivables did most of the work this year. Payable days are on COGS, but payables also include bills for advertising and services, so this flatters the payables side somewhat; the same bias applies to Marico.

**The ratio that misleads: ROCE.** Dabur's ROCE of 20% looks ordinary, but **₹9,489 cr, 54% of its total assets, is cash and investments**, and they earn interest, not operating profit. Other income was **24.8% of profit before tax** in FY26. If I take cash, bank balances and investments out of capital employed (₹12,865 cr − ₹9,489 cr = ₹3,377 cr), the operating business earns roughly 59% on what is left (₹1,983 cr operating profit ÷ ₹3,377 cr). That figure is sensitive to how much Dabur borrows and parks, so I treat it as an illustration, not a precise number. The headline ROCE is not wrong. It describes a mix of a very profitable consumer business and a large treasury earning bank-like returns.

## 5. Growth or profitability

Dabur is choosing profitability and safety, and the evidence says the profitability is slipping. Operating margin has fallen from **18.4% in FY22 to 16.1% in FY23 and FY24, 14.9% in FY25 and 15.0% in FY26**, while revenue grew only **4.9% a year** (FY22 from Screener aggregates; FY23 to FY26 from the audited statements). Gross margin held at about 48%, so the slide is in the costs below gross profit, not in pricing.

What it is choosing not to spend on: advertising is **6.7% of revenue** (Marico 9.6%), capex fell from ₹570 cr to ₹420 cr in FY26, and it declared **₹1,463 cr of dividends for FY26, 77% of owners' profit** (FY26 investor presentation), while still adding to a ₹9,489 cr investment book. Dabur is not burning cash; it is holding cash it has not found a use for.

## 6. How it is funded, and who carries the risk

Borrowings are **₹1,063 cr against owners' equity of ₹11,420 cr: debt ÷ equity 0.09** (0.11 if lease liabilities are added). Operating profit covers finance costs **13.6 times**. Against ₹9,489 cr of cash and investments, Dabur is **net cash of ₹8,426 cr**. Shareholders carry almost all the risk, and that risk is small.

The DuPont leverage factor of 1.53 is not borrowing: assets ÷ equity counts every liability, and the biggest one is ₹2,954 cr owed to suppliers, not ₹1,063 cr owed to banks.

The question I would put to management: **why carry ₹1,047 cr of short-term borrowings, at a finance cost of ₹145 cr (about 14% of the closing balance), while holding ₹9,489 cr in investments?** If it is subsidiaries borrowing locally overseas, the statements do not show which ones, and that is the disclosure I would ask for.
