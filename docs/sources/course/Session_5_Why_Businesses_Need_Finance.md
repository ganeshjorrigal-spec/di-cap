CURVEBALL 1  ·  RESULT

Stall | Break-even | Sales in the rain | Profit / loss

B · Vada pav | 110 | 108 | −₹12

[Notes] COMPUTED. Let them find their own number before you show this.

The question to ask: why did the stall with the BEST contribution margin do worst? Answer: it had the highest fixed costs — ₹3,000 against chai's ₹900. High fixed costs mean every unit of volume matters more.

This is operating leverage, and they have just derived it.

## Slide 16

OPERATING LEVERAGE
Why the best margin lost the most
Low fixed costs
Chai: ₹900 a day.

A bad day is a smaller good day. You bend.
High fixed costs
Cold coffee: ₹3,000 a day.

A bad day is a loss. You break.
Fixed costs multiply whatever happens to your volume — in both directions.
On a good day, cold coffee makes far more than chai. That is the same fact, read the other way. You do not get one without the other.

[Notes] Say the both-directions point explicitly. Students hear 'high fixed costs are bad' — they are not. They are a bet on volume.

Connect forward: Session 4's Blinkit question. Owning inventory and running dark stores is a fixed-cost bet on volume. This is why quick commerce either works enormously or not at all.

## Slide 18

CURVEBALL 2  ·  CHAI STALL
The deal, worked out
25% off the things that keep — tea, sugar, biscuits, cups — if you take three days' worth today. ₹1,800 now instead of ₹2,400 over three days. Milk is not in the deal. It will not keep.
 | Without the deal | With the deal
Variable cost per cup | ₹6 | ₹5
Contribution per cup | ₹9 | ₹10
Break-even | 100 cups | 90 cups
Profit at 200 cups | ₹900 | ₹1,100
Profit at 120 cups (rain) | ₹180 | ₹300
Better at every volume. Lower break-even. So why would anybody hesitate?

[Notes] CORRECTED. An earlier version of this slide treated the prepaid ingredients as a sunk fixed cost and showed break-even RISING. That was wrong.

Prepaid stock is inventory. If you do not sell it today you still have it tomorrow — nothing is lost. So the discount simply lowers your cost per cup, raises contribution from ₹9 to ₹10, and lowers break-even from 100 to 90. On PROFIT the deal is better at every single volume.

COMPUTED: storables are ₹4 of the ₹6 variable cost (tea and sugar ₹1.70, biscuit ₹1.50, cup and lid ₹0.80). Milk at ₹1.80 and the payment fee at ₹0.20 are not in the deal. 600 cups of storables at ₹4 = ₹2,400; at 25% off = ₹1,800, so ₹3 a cup instead of ₹4.

Let them conclude it is a good deal. It is. Then turn the page.

## Slide 19

CURVEBALL 2  ·  SO WHAT IS IT?
The catch is not in the profit
₹1,800 leaves today
Instead of about ₹800 a day as you go. The profit arrives over three days. The payment does not.
If it rains for three days
You sell 360 cups and bought stock for 600. Nothing is wasted — but ₹720 of your money is sitting in a sack in the storeroom instead of in your till.
And the milk is still daily

[Notes] NEW, and it is where curveball 2 actually pays off.

The deal improves profit and worsens cash. That is the session's entire theme arriving inside an operating decision, three slides before you name it.

COMPUTED: three rainy days sell 360 of the 600 cups bought; 240 cups of storables remain at ₹3 = ₹720 tied up.

The question to ask, and it is the real one: 'the deal is worth ₹600 over three days. Do you have ₹1,800 today?' Yesterday this stall closed ₹1,500 down. For a lot of small businesses the answer is no, and that is why they pay more per unit than larger competitors — not because they are worse at negotiating, but because they cannot afford the discount.

That last point is worth saying out loud. It is the clearest example in the course of why cash constraints make you poorer over time.

## Slide 21

CURVEBALL 3
A 10% price cut is never a 10% problem
Stall | Contribution | Falls by | Profit at normal volume | Falls by
A · Chai (60% margin) | ₹9 → ₹7.50 | 17% | ₹900 → ₹600 | 33%
C · Momos (50% margin) | ₹30 → ₹24 | 20% | ₹1,200 → ₹600 | 50%
E · Sandwich (52% margin) | ₹26 → ₹21 | 19% | ₹1,560 → ₹1,010 | 35%
10% off the price. Half the profit.
It hits twice. The discount comes entirely out of contribution, because your costs did not move — that is the first 17 to 20%. Then your fixed costs are unchanged and have to be covered out of less, which is what takes it to a third or a half.

[Notes] COMPUTED. An earlier version of this slide said '10% of the price, 20% of the profit'. That was wrong — 20% is the fall in CONTRIBUTION for momos. Profit falls by half, because fixed costs do not move.

Make them compute contribution first, then profit. The two-stage answer is the lesson: the price cut takes a bite out of contribution, and then operating leverage takes a second bite out of profit.

The general rule for the board: a price cut of x% cuts contribution by x ÷ margin%. At a 25% margin, a 10% cut destroys 40% of contribution — and far more of the profit.

Then ask: whose business can survive a discount war? Straight back to Session 4's quick-commerce question.

## Slide 22

Profit tells you what you earned.
Cash tells you what you can pay today.
Both numbers are true. The profit is real — it is just sitting in somebody else's pocket and in a sack of tea leaves.

[Notes] The sentence for the board. Leave it up for the rest of the session; it reappears in Sessions 6, 8 and 9.

You will meet the older aphorism — 'profit is an opinion, cash is a fact'. It is memorable and they will hear it in industry, so it is worth mentioning. But do not lead with it: 'opinion' makes beginners think profit is arbitrary, when it is a measure governed by rules and judgements. The earned-versus-payable version is what they need first.
