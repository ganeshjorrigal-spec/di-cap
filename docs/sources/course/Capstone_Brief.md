# The question

> **You have spent a term inside one company, and since Session 14 you
> have been measuring it against a peer. On the evidence about their
> business economics, cash generation, returns and financial risk —
> which would you back, and what would change your mind?**

That is the whole assignment. Everything below is about answering it
well.

**This is individual work.** Your company, your peer, your
recommendation.

> **What “back” means here.** You are judging which is the **stronger,
> more resilient business** — not making a buy-or-sell call on its
> shares at today’s price. A strong business can be a poor investment if
> its shares are expensive enough, and this course has not taught you
> how to value a company. If you mention the share price, treat it as a
> limitation of your argument, not as evidence for it.

# The written submission

**This is a correction and synthesis task, not new research.** Almost
everything in it comes from work you have already done. If you find
yourself starting a new line of research the week of the deadline, stop
and ask whether the argument needs it.

**Three parts.** Your company gets the full analysis. The peer gets a
shorter profile on the same measures. Then the argument.

## The ground rules for every part

| Rule                                      | What it means                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
|-------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Same years, same basis**                | Use the same financial years for both companies, and **consolidated** statements unless you say why not                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Every material claim has its evidence** | The number and its period; where it came from; how it was defined or calculated; why it supports the claim; and any important limitation                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| **Not every sentence needs a number**     | “It is a capital-intensive business” can rest on the business model. “Its margin is falling” needs the series. “It is falling *because of* discounting” needs evidence for the cause — not just the series                                                                                                                                     |
| **Gaps are allowed**                      | If a figure is **not disclosed**, or a ratio is **not meaningful** for your company — negative earnings, an unusual capital structure, a business where the usual ratio misleads — say so, say where you looked or why, and move on. **You are marked on your search and your reasoning, not on finding a number the company never published** |
| **Choose what matters**                   | Calculate the required ratios, but explain only the ones that matter to your argument                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |

| Part                  | Covers                                 | Length              |
|-----------------------|----------------------------------------|---------------------|
| **A — Your company**  | Elements 1 to 6, in full               | Three pages at most |
| **B — The peer**      | Element 7 — the same measures, shorter | One page            |
| **C — The argument**  | Elements 8 and 9                       | Two pages at most   |

## Part A — Your company

### 1 · What it sells, and to whom

Two or three sentences, no jargon. If you cannot say it plainly, you
have not understood it yet.

**Include:** the main products or services, the main customers, and the
segment that is growing fastest — with its revenue, from the segment
note.

**The common mistake:** copying the company’s own description from its
website. “A leading provider of integrated solutions” tells the reader
nothing.

### 2 · How it makes money on each unit

What one unit is — an order, a customer, a tonne, a subscription, a loan
— and roughly what is left from it after the costs that come with it.

**Include:** the unit, and what you can find about revenue and cost per
unit. Then the one cost choice that explains the margin — the middle box
from the financial story: *business model → cost structure → margin.*

**The common mistake:** stating the margin without naming the cost
choice that causes it. “Margins are low” describes. “Every order needs a
rider, and a rider does not get cheaper with scale” explains.

> **Per-unit figures are often not disclosed.** If they are not, say so,
> say where you looked — the annual report, the quarterly shareholder
> letter, the investor presentation — and work with what is there.
> **“Not disclosed” with evidence is a full answer. An invented per-unit
> number is zero.**

### 3 · Whether its profit becomes cash

**Include:** three consecutive financial years of net profit against
cash from operations, on the same basis. The **largest material item**
that explains the gap — and what it tells you, not just its name. And
what the company did with the cash — capital spending, dividends,
repaying borrowings, or buying something.

**If the statements do not make the cause clear, say so.** “The largest
item is the change in receivables; the annual report does not say why
receivables rose” is a complete answer.

**The common mistake:** reporting cash from operations without saying
why it differs from profit. The difference is the analysis.

### 4 · What its ratios say — and where they mislead

**Include:** operating margin, net margin, ROCE, ROE and the DuPont
decomposition, for the latest year and at least one earlier year. Then
**one ratio that could mislead a reader** about your company, and why.
If a ratio is not meaningful for your company, say which and why
instead.

**State your definitions.** Which equity, which profit, consolidated or
standalone. One line.

**Use the course’s DuPont convention — closing balances, and write the
formula out:**

> **ROE = (net profit ÷ revenue) × (revenue ÷ closing total assets) ×
> (closing total assets ÷ closing equity)**

With closing balances throughout, the three factors multiply to exactly
net profit ÷ closing equity. **If they do not, it is arithmetic.** If
you choose average balances instead, say so and use them everywhere —
and then compare with ROE on average equity, not closing.

### 5 · Growth or profitability

Which is your company pursuing, and does the evidence say it is working?

**Include:** the direction of the operating margin over at least three
years, not one year’s level. If the company is burning cash, whether the
loss is shrinking per unit and what it is buying. If it is profitable,
what it is choosing *not* to spend on.

**The common mistake:** “They are investing in growth.” In what, how
much, and what has it returned so far?

### 6 · How it is funded, and who carries the risk

**Include:** borrowings against equity from the balance sheet, interest
cover, and **one sentence on why the company chose that mix** — or a
question you would put to management if you cannot tell.

**The common mistake:** confusing the DuPont leverage factor with
borrowing. Assets ÷ equity counts every liability. Debt ÷ equity counts
only the borrowings. Know which one you are quoting.

## Part B — The peer

### 7 · The peer, on the same measures

**One page.** You do not need to repeat the full analysis — you need
enough to compare fairly.

| Include                                                   | Where it comes from                          |
|-----------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| What it sells, and to whom — two sentences                | Its annual report                            |
| Operating margin, net margin, ROCE, ROE and DuPont        | **Your ratio scorecard — already done**      |
| The cash conversion cycle                                 | **Your ratio scorecard — already done**      |
| Net profit against cash from operations, latest two years | Its cash flow statement — two numbers a year |
| Borrowings ÷ equity, and interest cover, latest year      | Its balance sheet and P&L                    |

**Use the same definitions for both companies.** If you used closing
equity for yours, use closing equity for the peer. A comparison built on
two different definitions is not a comparison.

## Part C — The argument

### 8 · Which one you would back, and on what conditions

**Pick one.** “Both have strengths” is not a recommendation.

**Include:** the single difference between the two businesses that
decides it for you, three numbers that support it, and **the
conditions** — what has to stay true for your recommendation to hold.

**Backing a company is not the same as saying it is bigger, more
profitable or more famous.** It means: on the evidence about how the two
businesses make money, turn profit into cash, earn returns and carry
risk — this one is stronger, and here is why. **You may back the peer
over your own company** — some of the best capstones will.

### 9 · The strongest case against your recommendation

Write the argument someone would make if they wanted to back the *other*
company — and make it a good one. Then answer it.

**End with the number that would change your mind**, and a date: *“If
\[this company\]’s operating margin is not above X% by FY28, I would
switch.”*

**This is the hardest element, and it carries real marks.** Anyone can
collect numbers that agree with them. The skill this course has been
building is knowing what would prove you wrong.

presented as a fact is not.

# The presentation

**Three minutes, hard stop. Then one question, about one minute.**

**Four slides at most after the title.** This is the structure:

| Slide | What it does                                                                        | Time |
|-------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------|
| Title | Your company, your peer, the industry, your name                                    | —    |
| **1** | The question, and **the one difference that matters most**                          | 0:40 |
| **2** | The evidence — three numbers, **both companies side by side**                       | 1:10 |
| **3** | Your recommendation, and its conditions                                             | 0:35 |
| **4** | The strongest case against you, and the number and date that would change your mind | 0:35 |

**Three minutes is short on purpose.** The written submission carries
the full analysis. The presentation carries only the argument — and an
argument that cannot be made in three minutes is usually not yet clear
to the person making it.

**Your instructor will select and ask one question** — about your
company, your peer or your argument. Answer it yourself. That answer is
a fifth of your presentation mark.

**It tests understanding, not memory.** If you cannot recall an exact
figure, you can say how you calculated it and which statement or note
holds the inputs — that is a good answer.

**An assigned classmate listens to your presentation** and may write a
question on a card while you speak. Your instructor may use it, adapt it
or ask a different one. **Your mark never depends on the quality of a
classmate’s question.**

**Timing:** a card goes up at **2:30**. At **3:00** you stop. If you run
over, you lose your own presentation time — never the next student’s.

- **Upload one PDF to DOT 24 hours before Session 18** — whether you

# The personal reflection

**One page.** Due with the written submission. Answer three questions:

1.  **One financial decision you now make differently** because of this
    course — about your own money, not a company’s. Be specific: what
    you did before, what you do now, and why. **If you have not yet had
    the chance to act on it, describe the specific change you will make,
    and when** — that is a full answer.
2.  **Your mock portfolio.** What happened to it, what you changed and
    why — and **how much of the result was your reasoning and how much
    was luck?** A portfolio that went up is not evidence of a good
    decision. Tell us how you would know the difference.
3.  **The question you would investigate next** if you had another term
    with your company.

A few of you will be asked to read yours aloud at the end of Session 19.

# Rules that apply to every number

1.  **Every figure from a statement says which statement and which
    year.**
2.  **State your definitions — and use the same ones for both
    companies.**
3.  **A ratio from a website must say so**, and you must not compare it
    with one you calculated yourself unless you know they were built the
    same way.
4.  **“Cannot determine from the published statements” is an acceptable
    answer**, if you say what is missing and where you looked.
5.  **Never invent a number.** An assumption is fine if it is labelled
    as yours.

# What strong and weak look like

| Weak                                                 | Strong                                                                                                                                             |
|------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| “My company is better because it has higher revenue” | “A converts more of its profit into cash — 94% against 61% over three years — because it collects from customers in 12 days and its peer takes 48” |
| “Both companies have strengths and weaknesses”       | “I back A, on one condition: that its margin holds above 8% while it opens stores. If it falls below 6% by FY28, I switch”                         |
| A slide of eleven ratios                             | Three numbers, each with the reason it matters                                                                                                     |
| “The peer’s ROE is higher, so the peer is better”    | “The peer’s ROE is higher because its equity is small, not because its business earns more — its ROCE is lower than A’s”                           |
| “There are no risks”                                 | “The strongest case for the peer is its growth. Here is why I still do not think it is enough”                                                     |
| A figure with no source                              | A figure with its statement, its year, and whether it was reported or calculated                                                                   |
