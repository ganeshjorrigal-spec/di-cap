Business Finance - S16
Unit Economics, Channel Margins, Debt and Equity

1. Read the Business Through Connected Statements
The session opens with students identifying their biggest learnings and areas needing
revision. Cash flow, working capital, annual reports, ratios and the connections between the
P&L, balance sheet and cash-flow statement emerge repeatedly. The instructor encourages
peer discussion and following the movement of money to understand these relationships.
The main discussion then examines two managerial questions: how to compare business
channels meaningfully, and how to choose between borrowing and raising equity. Both
require looking beyond one headline number. Profitability, cash generation, timing, existing
obligations and ownership consequences shape the decision together.

2. Understand Contribution Margins as Layers of the P&L
Gross profit shows what remains after the costs included in the business's cost-of-goods
calculation. Contribution measures such as CM1, CM2 and CM3 provide further stages of
profitability by deducting additional groups of expenses. They help managers see where
value is being retained or spent.
In the classroom illustration, channel-related costs appear in an earlier layer, performance
marketing in a later layer and brand marketing in another. The instructor explicitly notes that
these labels are not defined identically by every company. A business may classify costs
differently from another business using the same CM label.
•     Ask what expenses are included before comparing contribution measures.
•     Use the layers to understand the operating model and major cost drivers.
•     Do not treat an internal contribution label as a universally standard definition.
The purpose is a more useful view of the P&L, including communication with investors. A
short set of contribution lines can summarise many expense categories, but it remains
necessary to understand the detail behind them.

3. Separate MRP, Customer Payment and Net Revenue
The instructor starts with a product carrying an MRP of Rs 100. If the seller gives a Rs 20
discount, the customer pays Rs 80. The MRP is therefore not the amount that should
automatically be treated as the seller's net revenue.
The example also assumes the Rs 80 price includes GST at 18%. To extract the
tax-exclusive amount, divide by 1.18 rather than subtracting 18% of Rs 80. The tax
percentage applies to the underlying taxable value, while the displayed price already
includes it.
Illustrative calculation: Tax-exclusive value = Rs 80 / 1.18 = approximately Rs 67.80. The
included tax is approximately Rs 12.20. The class rounds the net-revenue figure to about Rs
68 for the subsequent comparison.
The rate is an assumption in the exercise. The learning is the calculation method and the
distinction between a tax-inclusive receipt and revenue belonging to the business.
4. Trace Who Is Buying from the Business
The channel comparison turns on the identity of the seller's customer. In the marketplace
illustration, the brand sells to the end consumer through a platform. The seller records the
relevant sale and the platform charges a separate fee, shown among expenses in the
example.
In the inventory-led illustration, the brand sells to the channel partner, which then sells to the
consumer. The partner's commercial margin affects the amount at which the brand sells to
that partner. Further brand-funded discounts can also reduce what the brand ultimately
retains.
Amazon and Blinkit are used as classroom examples to demonstrate these structures. The
instructor emphasises that actual arrangements depend on the business and partner. The
lesson is to inspect the transaction and commercial terms rather than assume a platform
name determines the accounting treatment in every case.

5. Compare Contribution Rupees Alongside Margin Percentages
Margin expresses profit or contribution as a percentage of the relevant revenue base. If two
channels record different revenue amounts for the same product, a similar percentage can
correspond to very different contribution in rupees.
Using the class's rounded marketplace figures: net revenue of Rs 68, product cost of Rs 20,
logistics of Rs 5 and platform fee of Rs 18 leave Rs 25 at the contribution layer being
discussed. The contribution margin is approximately 36.8% of Rs 68.
In the second simplified channel example, the class arrives at net revenue of Rs 40 after the
commercial deductions discussed. With product cost of Rs 20 and logistics of Rs 5,
contribution is Rs 15, or 37.5% of net revenue.
The percentages are close, but the same product contributes Rs 25 in one example and Rs
15 in the other. The lower percentage can therefore accompany the higher contribution in
rupees. These are rounded teaching examples, not published channel economics or a
complete tax reconciliation.

6. Use a Comparable Unit to Evaluate Economics
Unit economics asks what the business earns or contributes for a comparable unit, such as
the same product sold once. The comparison should keep the underlying unit and scope of
costs consistent. Otherwise a change in revenue presentation may be mistaken for a change
in economic attractiveness.
The instructor warns against abandoning a channel simply because its margin percentage
falls below another channel's. If its contribution per comparable unit remains higher, shifting
business could reduce the total contribution generated.
•     Identify the unit being compared.
•     Check net revenue and the costs included at the chosen contribution level.
•     Compare contribution in rupees as well as the percentage.
•     Consider volume, payment timing and the wider business model.
This contribution is not necessarily final net profit or cash in the bank. Further expenses can
remain below the contribution line, while collection and payment terms determine when
money moves.
7. Keep GST and Cash Movement Distinct from P&L Profit
For the simplified discussion, the instructor treats P&L revenue and costs as excluding GST
where the tax is recoverable. Tax collected from a customer is not simply additional business
income. Likewise, a vendor invoice can require a cash payment above the underlying cost
recorded in the P&L.
The class uses an illustration in which a vendor receives Rs 25: Rs 20 for the product and Rs
5 identified as tax. The product cost and total cash outflow therefore differ. The example is
designed to explain the separation, not establish a tax rate for that product.
Input tax credit is introduced as the mechanism through which eligible tax paid on purchases
can offset tax collected on sales. Detailed eligibility, unrecoverable tax and differences in
rates are outside this session's simplified treatment.
The practical distinction is that revenue, expense and cash movement need to be read
together. A profitable transaction can still create a funding requirement if cash goes out
before the sale proceeds arrive.

8. Include Cash Timing in the Channel Decision
A student asks whether the channel producing more contribution also produces better cash
flow. The instructor agrees that collection timing matters but does not establish a universal
payment advantage for either channel. For the worked comparison, timing is assumed equal.
The fuller decision includes how much contribution is generated and when the related cash
is collected. Working capital, settlement cycles and the cost of funding a delay can change
the result. This connects the new discussion with the earlier lessons on cash flow and
financial-statement movements.

9. Debt Creates Obligations; Equity Shares Ownership
Borrowing requires the business to meet agreed interest and principal payments. Those
obligations continue through stronger and weaker periods. Loan covenants and any security
provided also affect the risk of borrowing.
Equity brings an investor into ownership. In the simple comparison, the business does not
promise the investor the same fixed repayment schedule as a lender, but existing owners
give up a share of the business and its future upside. Equity therefore has a cost even when
there is no recurring interest payment.
The relevant question is whether the business can sustain the obligations it is taking on and
whether the ownership trade-off is acceptable. A mix of funding sources may be appropriate;
the session does not present one source as best for every company.

10. Case One: A Stable Cold-Storage Business
The first hypothetical business has Rs 200 crore of revenue, Rs 30 crore of operating profit,
a six-year track record and long-term contracts with two dairy companies. It needs Rs 100
crore to expand its warehouses. Contracted revenue and a more established operating
history make borrowing worth considering.
However, the class tests whether taking the full amount as debt leaves enough room for
problems. At an illustrative 10% interest rate, Rs 100 crore of debt creates Rs 10 crore of
annual interest. Adding an assumed Rs 10 crore of annual principal repayment creates Rs
20 crore of debt service.
That is substantial against Rs 30 crore of operating profit, particularly if expansion is
delayed. Operating profit is not automatically cash available for repayment. The discussion
therefore considers a largely debt-funded approach while recognising the possible need for a
mix and a financial buffer.

11. Case Two: A Loss-Making Growth Business
The second business has doubled revenue to Rs 200 crore but is making a Rs 40 crore loss.
It wants to open 60 stores, and none of its existing stores is older than 18 months. The
operating model has limited maturity and uncertain near-term cash generation.
The class leans toward equity because adding fixed repayment obligations would increase
pressure on a business already losing money. An equity investor would still examine the
path to profitability, the time required and the risk that the business does not achieve its plan.
Revenue growth alone does not prove that the business can safely service debt. The funding
decision must account for the stage of the business, the reliability of its economics and the
uncertainty in future cash flows.

12. Calculate Pre-Money and Post-Money Valuation
The growth-business exercise assumes an investor puts Rs 100 crore into the company for a
20% ownership stake after the investment. This implies a post-money valuation of Rs 500
crore: Rs 100 crore divided by 20%.
The pre-money valuation is Rs 400 crore: post-money value of Rs 500 crore minus the Rs
100 crore of fresh investment. The existing owners hold the remaining 80% after the
transaction in this simplified example.
Post-money valuation = Investment / Post-investment ownership percentage.
Pre-money valuation = Post-money valuation - Fresh investment.
The instructor uses an orchard analogy to separate the value of the existing business from
the new cash invested. The agreed valuation and ownership percentage are negotiable.
Revenue of Rs 200 crore does not itself establish a Rs 200 crore company valuation.

13. Case Three: An Exporter Already Under Debt Pressure
The final hypothetical business has Rs 200 crore of revenue and Rs 30 crore of operating
profit, but performance is declining. It already owes banks Rs 150 crore, with Rs 90 crore
due within two years. Its funding problem differs from that of a healthy business planning
expansion.
Further borrowing can deepen the pressure. An equity investor may also be difficult to attract
and may seek a large ownership stake, stronger control or management changes as part of
a restructuring. Waiting until the business is distressed can weaken its negotiating position.
The closing message is to connect the P&L, balance sheet, cash flow, unit economics and
financing obligations. Returns relative to borrowing cost matter, but so do repayment
capacity, volatility and the existing debt burden. The complete picture is more useful than
any single ratio.
Key Takeaways
1. Check how contribution metrics are defined before comparing companies or channels.
2. MRP, tax-inclusive customer receipts and net revenue are different amounts.
3. Compare contribution per unit alongside margin percentage.
4. Include cash timing and working-capital needs in the decision.
5. Match funding obligations to the stability and maturity of the business.
6. Equity reduces existing owners' percentage ownership; debt creates repayment
commitments.
7. Separate pre-money business value from post-money value after fresh investment.
