## Slide 3

FROM SESSION 2
You already know the equation
Net worth  =  what you own  −  what you owe
Move one term across:
Assets  =  Liabilities  +  Equity
Same sentence, rearranged. Not a rule somebody invented — it is arithmetic, and it cannot fail to balance.

[Notes] Do the rearrangement on the board. Students find the accounting equation mysterious; it is the net worth formula they computed for themselves in Session 2.\n\nSay explicitly: it balances because it is a definition, not because accountants are careful.

## Slide 4

How a real one is arranged
ASSETS
Non-current — things that stay
Property · plant · equipment · long-term investments · goodwill

Current — things that turn over within a year
Stock · money owed to us · cash
LIABILITIES + EQUITY
Equity — the owners' share
What was put in · profits kept in the business

Non-current liabilities
Long-term borrowings

Current liabilities
Suppliers · short-term debt · tax due
Current assets minus current liabilities is working capital — the number you met at the stall, now with an address.

[Notes] Current means within twelve months. Everything liquid sits at the bottom of assets and the bottom of liabilities, which is where you look first.\n\nAsk: which side does a session pass sit on for the class store? Liability — money taken for something not yet delivered. They have been on both sides of that one.

## Slide 5

GROSS DEBT vs NET DEBT
One number, two ways of saying it
Reliance Industries, as at 31 March 2020:
₹3.36 lakh cr
Gross debt — what it owed
₹1.75 lakh cr
Cash it was holding
₹1.61 lakh cr
Net debt — the difference
Both numbers are true. They tell you different things.
Net debt asks
If you used your cash to pay down debt, what would be left?
Gross debt asks
What do you actually owe, and to whom, and when?

[Notes] VERIFIED: gross debt ₹3.36 trillion, cash ₹1.75 trillion, net debt ₹1.61 trillion as at 31 March 2020.\n\nNet debt is the cleaner measure of financial strength; gross debt is what has to be refinanced. A company with huge cash and huge debt still has to roll the debt over, and the cash may be trapped in subsidiaries or other countries.

## Slide 7

THE CAVEAT
Now read it like an analyst
Of the ₹53,124 crore rights issue, only 25% — about ₹13,281 crore — was actually received in that financial year. The remaining 75% came the following year.
The full amount was counted in the announcement. Every rupee was legally committed, and shareholders had signed up — but it had not arrived.
Is the claim wrong?
No. Committed capital is real, and the company was open about the phasing.
Is it the whole picture?
Also no. 'Net debt free' on the day meant a promise of cash, not cash in the account.
Committed is not collected. You learned that at a chai stall — the office next door had committed too.

[Notes] VERIFIED, and the best part of the case. Do not present it as a gotcha — Reliance disclosed the phasing and the deals were genuine.\n\nThe lesson is about reading. A headline balance-sheet claim can be technically accurate and still require you to check when the money arrives. Same distinction as the stall, five orders of magnitude larger.\n\nWorth adding: the net-debt-free status did not last. The company took on debt again for expansion. A balance sheet is a photograph of one day, and somebody chose the day.

## Slide 19

THE STRUCTURE
Three piles, and the test for each
OPERATING
Did running the business generate cash?
Everything to do with selling the thing you sell, and paying for it. Customers, suppliers, staff, stock, rent, tax.
INVESTING
What did it spend on its future — or sell off?
Buying and selling long-lived things. Equipment, factories, other companies. Also where treasury income and deposits show up.
FINANCING
Who funded the gap, and what did they charge?
Money from and to owners and lenders. Share issues, borrowings, repayments, interest, dividends.
The three add up to one number: the change in the bank balance. That number cannot be argued with.

## Slide 26

55–62 MIN  ·  WHY COMPANIES FAIL
Two questions to ask of that statement
Take the ₹1,00,000 away
The business stops at transaction 2. It cannot buy the equipment. Not because it was unprofitable — because the cash to trade arrived from outside, and outside can stop.
Now double everything
Twice the stock, twice the sales, twice the credit given. Operating goes from (5,000) to (10,000). The hole scales with the business. Session 5, with a statement attached.
A company fails when it runs out of cash, not when it runs out of profit
Profit is an opinion about timing. Cash is a bank balance. Only one of the two can refuse to pay a salary.

## Slide 27

ANATOMY  ·  THE INDIRECT METHOD
What a real one adds
Line you will see | What it is doing
Profit before tax | The starting point. Before tax, not after
Add: depreciation and amortisation | Removing a charge that moved no cash this year
Add: finance costs | Taken out here, shown again under financing
Less: other income / interest income | Not from operations. Reappears under investing
Changes in receivables, inventories, payables | The promises, undone
Less: taxes paid | Cash paid to the government, not the tax charge in the P&L
Net cash from operating activities | The single most useful number in the annual report
Depreciation is added back because no cash left the building this year — it left years ago, when the machine was bought. It does not create cash. “Costs no cash” and “costs nothing” are still different sentences.

## Slide 28

READING ONE IN TEN SECONDS
Four sign patterns worth recognising
Op | Inv | Fin | What it usually means | Ask
+ | − | − | Earns cash, invests some, returns the rest | Is the reinvestment enough?
+ | − | + | Earns cash but borrows for its ambitions | Can it service what it raised?
− | − | + | Funded by investors. Most young companies | How long does the money last?
+ | + | − | Selling assets and paying down debt | Selling by choice, or by necessity?
None of these four is a verdict. Each is a question you now know how to ask. The third shape describes both a healthy company two years from profitability and a company about to go under — the statement will not tell you which. The rest of this course is about telling them apart.

## Slide 31

70–76 MIN  ·  THE BRIDGE
A lower day-count is not always better
Days | Divided by | FY25 | FY26 | Effect on the cycle
Receivable days | revenue | 35 | 12 | (23)  better
Inventory days | purchases | 12 | 26 | +15  worse
Payable days | purchases | 101 | 36 | +64  worse
Cash conversion cycle |  | (54) | +2 | +56 days
Receivable days falling is good
Less cash tied up per rupee sold. Receivables actually fell ₹182 cr while reported revenue nearly tripled.
Payable days falling is not
Less supplier credit per rupee bought. At FY25's 101 days, payables would be ₹8,327 cr, not ₹3,010 cr.
Two of the three make the cycle better when they fall. The third makes it worse, and it did most of the moving.

## Slide 32

ANSWER THESE THREE IN YOUR OWN WORDS
A negative cycle is a loan nobody charges you for
1
Who is financing a business whose suppliers are paid after its customers pay?
2
Receivable days fell and payable days fell. Why is only one of those good news?
3
The cycle got 56 days worse and cash from operations doubled. How can both be true?
No hands up until somebody has used the word “float” without me saying it first.
The mechanism is real
When customers pay before suppliers do, the supplier lends you money interest-free. Grow and the float grows. Shrink and it is repaid out of a falling revenue base — ask any retailer who has closed stores.
Question 3 has an answer
Profit before tax actually fell, ₹697 cr to ₹615 cr. Cash from operations doubled because the depreciation added back nearly doubled — ₹863 cr to ₹1,597 cr. They built stores.
Read the components. The cycle is a summary, and summaries hide things

## Slide 35

BYJU'S  ·  THE REAL LESSON
Where was the cash flow statement?
The revenue question
Course fees collected upfront for a multi-year product. When should that be revenue? The audited FY21 accounts spread it; the company had publicly guided to a much larger figure. The gap is the accounting policy, not the business.
The question nobody could answer
For two consecutive years there was no audited cash flow statement in the public record. Investors, lenders and journalists argued about revenue because the one statement that settles the argument had not been filed.
A statement you cannot see cannot reassure you. The delay was the signal
Ask the room: if you had been a lender in early 2022 with no audited accounts for twenty-two months, what would you have done — and on what evidence? That is the whole session in one question, and the answer is not "read the P&L more carefully."
