## Slide 3

ACT 1  ·  THE PUZZLE
Sixty seconds. Vote before you divide.
You have ₹100 to put into each of two businesses. Which is the better one?
 | Company A | Company B
Keeps, out of every ₹100 of sales | ₹15 | ₹8
Sales produced by every ₹1 of assets | ₹1.20 | ₹2.00
Return on assets | ? | ?
Hands up for A. Hands up for B. Write your choice down before anyone calculates anything — you will want to remember what you thought.
Almost everybody picks A. A keeps nearly twice as much of every sale.

## Slide 4

ACT 1  ·  THE REVEAL
A earns 18%. B earns 16%.
 | Company A | Company B
Margin | 15% | 8%
Sales per ₹1 of assets | × 1.20 | × 2.00
Return on assets | 18% | 16%
Constructed figures. 15% × 1.20 = 18%. 8% × 2.00 = 16%.
The company with almost half the margin is almost as profitable on its assets — and nothing whatsoever has been said about what either business sells.
A higher margin does not mean a better return. That gap is the whole session.

## Slide 6

ACT 2  ·  NAMING THEM
Three margins, three different questions
 | Formula | The question it answers
Gross margin | (Revenue − cost of goods sold) ÷ revenue | How much is left after making the thing?
Operating margin | Operating profit ÷ revenue | Does the business work before financing and tax?
Net margin | Net profit ÷ revenue | What survives everything?
Gross margin has a problem, and you already know what it is
An Indian statutory P&L groups expenses by nature, not by function. There is no “cost of goods sold” line to subtract — you build one, and the analyst next to you builds a different one.
So operating margin is usually the better starting point for comparing companies — but check what each one has put inside operating profit before you trust the comparison. Exceptional items and classification choices move it too.

One sells three times as much and earns less. One has five times the equity. Four numbers each, and you still cannot rank them — because a level tells you how big something is, never how good it is. Ratios are what you reach for when you want to compare rather than measure.

## Slide 8

ACT 3  ·  RETURN ON WHOSE MONEY?
A margin is not a return
A margin asks: of what came in, how much stayed?   A return asks: of what was put in, how much came back?
One company. ₹1,000 of assets. ₹150 of operating profit. Before any formula, answer these four:
Who put the ₹1,000 in? All owners, or owners and lenders?
If some of it was borrowed, who gets paid first out of the ₹150?
What is left for the owners once the lenders have been paid?
So which profit figure belongs on top of which pile of capital?
Every return in finance is one of those two piles divided into the profit that belongs to it.
Get the pair wrong and the number means nothing.

## Slide 9

ACT 3  ·  THE DEFINITIONS WE USE TODAY
Three returns, three different owners
 | Numerator | Denominator | Whose return is it?
ROE | Net profit | Shareholders' equity | The owners'
ROCE | Operating profit | Equity + borrowings | Everybody who put capital in
ROA | Net profit | Total assets | Per rupee of stuff the business holds
ROCE asks what all the money earned. ROE asks what just our money earned.
The numerator and the denominator must belong to the same people. Net profit is what is left after the lenders are paid, so it sits over equity — not over equity plus debt.
These are the definitions we use today, and they are on your handout. In practice ROCE is often EBIT over a defined capital-employed figure, and many analysts use average rather than closing balances. Check how both halves were built before comparing anyone's ROCE with anyone else's.

## Slide 10

ACT 4  ·  THE SAME BUSINESS, TWICE
Borrow, and your ROE goes up
₹, same business, two balance sheets | No debt | Half debt
Assets | 1,000 | 1,000
Debt at 8% | — | 500
Equity | 1,000 | 500
Operating profit | 150 | 150
Less: interest | — | (40)
Profit for shareholders | 150 | 110
ROE | 15.0% | 22.0%
ROCE | 15.0% | 15.0%
Constructed figures, chosen so the arithmetic is clean. Interest is 8% of ₹500.
Nothing about the business changed. ROE rose seven points; ROCE did not move at all.
Now do the bad year: operating profit is ₹40, not ₹150. Two minutes, both columns.

## Slide 11

ACT 4  ·  OPERATING PROFIT OF ₹40
Now the year it goes wrong
₹ | No debt | Half debt
Operating profit | 40 | 40
Less: interest | — | (40)
Profit for shareholders | 40 | —
ROE | 4.0% | 0.0%
ROCE | 4.0% | 4.0%
In the good year the borrower earned 22% against 15%. In the bad year the borrower earned nothing at all while the owner-funded business still earned 4%. The interest did not get smaller because the profit did.
Leverage does not create return. It widens it — in both directions.

## Slide 12

ACT 5  ·  BEFORE ANY ALGEBRA
Two businesses. Identical ROE.
 | Business A | Business B
Keeps per ₹1 of sales | 12% | 3%
Sales per ₹1 of assets | 1.0 × | 3.0 ×
Assets per ₹1 of equity | 1.5 × | 2.0 ×
Return on equity | 18% | 18%
Constructed. 12% × 1.0 × 1.5 = 18%. 3% × 3.0 × 2.0 = 18%.
Same headline number. One keeps four times as much of every sale; the other turns its assets over three times as fast and leans harder on money that is not its own. Would you say these two face the same risks?

## Slide 13

ACT 5  ·  DERIVE IT, DO NOT MEMORISE IT
Now take ROE apart
Start with what you want
ROE  =  Net profit  ÷  Equity
Multiply by revenue over revenue, and assets over assets. Both are 1, so nothing has changed
ROE  =  (Net profit ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Assets) × (Assets ÷ Equity)
Cancel, and check it is still the same thing
Revenue cancels. Assets cancel. Net profit ÷ Equity remains
Margin   ×   Turnover   ×   Leverage

## Slide 14

ACT 5  ·  DUPONT, IN WORDS
Three different ways to be profitable
 | What it measures | A business that lives here
Net margin | How much of each rupee of sales you keep | A luxury brand, a patented drug, a software licence
Asset turnover | How many rupees of sales each rupee of assets produces | A supermarket, a distributor, a restaurant that seats its tables three times
Leverage | How much of the assets was funded by somebody else | A bank, a property developer, an infrastructure firm
A table costs the same whether you seat it once an evening or three times. That is asset turnover — and the restaurant trade already calls it table turnover. Same word, same idea.
Your Business A and Business B were the two restaurants. The single 18% hid both of them. DuPont does not tell you which is better — it tells you which question to ask next.
Nobody serious quotes an ROE without saying where it came from.

## Slide 16

ACT 6  ·  THE ANSWER
Same country, same year, same shelf
FY26 | Avenue Supermarts | Nestlé India
Net margin | 4.3% | 15.1%
Asset turnover | 2.33 | 1.76
Leverage (assets ÷ equity) | 1.21 | 2.56
ROE | 12.1% | 67.8%
Computed from the four lines; both tie exactly to net profit ÷ equity.
Nestlé on margin and leverage.
DMart on turnover.
Three factors and they do not all point one way. Nestlé keeps three and a half times as much per rupee of sales. DMart turns its assets over about a third faster.
Leverage is not a debt ratio
Assets ÷ equity  =  1 + (liabilities ÷ equity). So Nestlé's 2.56 says its liabilities are 1.56 times its equity. Whether those are borrowings or unpaid suppliers, these four lines cannot tell you. The balance sheet can.

## Slide 18

ACT 7  ·  WHEN RATIOS MISLEAD
Watch a DuPont move without moving
Eternal | FY25 | FY26 | What happened
Net margin | 2.60% | 0.67% | Fell by three-quarters
Asset turnover | 0.57 | 1.33 | More than doubled
Leverage | 1.18 | 1.32 | Edged up
ROE | 1.74% | 1.18% | Fell
Computed from the FY26 annual report, consolidated and audited: revenue, profit, total assets and total equity.
Two of the three moved violently in opposite directions, and you know why: Blinkit began reporting the full value of goods sold instead of a commission. Revenue is the numerator of turnover and the denominator of margin, so one presentation change pushed them apart.
A change in how revenue is presented can move several ratios at once. Find out what changed in the accounting before you read it as the business improving or deteriorating.

## Slide 19

ACT 7  ·  AND THAT IS THE LESSON
Your ROE will not match the website's
Eternal, the same company, the same day | ROE
Your calculation: FY26 net profit ÷ FY26 closing equity | 1.18%
Screener's headline ratio box | 7.46%
Both pulled 10 October 2026. This is an unreconciled difference. I have not established what produces 7.46%, and neither should you guess.
Any of these could explain it. Finding out which is the exercise.
Opening equity, closing equity, or the average of the two
A multi-year average rather than a single year
Profit before or after exceptional items, and before or after minority interests
Standalone accounts rather than consolidated
A ratio without its definition is not a number. State yours, use it consistently, and never compare it with somebody else's until you know they built it the same way.

## Slide 20

ACT 8  ·  THE MAP, NOT A NEW LECTURE
Four families. You have used three.
Family | Asks | The two to know | Met it in
Profitability | Is it worth doing? | Operating margin · ROE | Today
Liquidity | Can it pay next month? | Current ratio · quick ratio | Balance sheet
Efficiency | How hard do the assets work? | Asset turnover · the cash cycle | Cash flow · today
Leverage | Who really owns this? | Debt ÷ equity · interest cover | Still to come
You built the current ratio's two columns in the balance-sheet session without naming the division, and the cash conversion cycle is an efficiency ratio counted in days instead of times. Nothing here is new. It now has labels.
No single ratio is a verdict, and a ratio outside its industry is barely a fact.
