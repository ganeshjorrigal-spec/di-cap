## Slide 3

5–8 MIN  ·  THE QUESTION
Why are investors willing to fund
companies that lose money?
One company in this room's syllabus lost ₹1,222 crore in a year and is now worth over ₹3 lakh crore. Another lost ₹4,154 crore last year and raised more. A third lost ₹8,245 crore, and its founder later said it was worth zero. The losses do not tell them apart.

## Slide 4

13–20 MIN  ·  WHAT IS ACTUALLY BEING BOUGHT
Nobody is funding the loss
What the P&L shows | What the investor is pricing
This year's loss | A share of a market that does not exist yet
Today's revenue | Revenue at a scale where the fixed costs are spread
Today's margin | The margin once the discounting stops
Cash going out | An asset being built — a network, a habit, a brand
Every item in the right-hand column is an assumption. None of them is audited, none of them is in any statement, and the entire disagreement between Damodaran and Gurley lived in the first row.
Which means the question is never “is it losing money?” It is “what has to be true for this to work, and is it becoming true?”

## Slide 5

20–24 MIN  ·  THE MECHANICS
Two words you will hear in every raise
BURN
Cash out minus cash in, per month
Not the loss. Cash.
RUNWAY
Cash in the bank ÷ monthly burn
Months until you cannot pay anyone.
The mistake almost everybody makes
They compute burn from the profit and loss account. Depreciation is in it and is not cash. Capital expenditure is cash and is not in it. And a change in working capital moves operating cash without moving profit by the same amount — which is why it sits on the cash flow statement and not the P&L. You met all three in Session 11.
Burn is a cash flow statement number. If you took it off a P&L, it is wrong.
And operating burn is not runway. Runway has to carry the capital spending, the working capital and the timing of payments as well.

## Slide 6

24–32 MIN  ·  WORK IT
A startup with ₹40 lakh in the bank
Year 1 plan | ₹ lakh
Revenue | 60
Cash operating costs | (96)
Depreciation | (6)
Net loss | (42)
Equipment bought in month 1, cash | (24)

Three minutes. Find the real one
What is the actual monthly cash burn?
When does the money run out?

## Slide 7

THE ANSWER
Eleven months becomes five
Step | Working | ₹ lakh
Operating cash burn, per year | 60 revenue − 96 cash costs. Depreciation is not cash | (36)
Per month | 36 ÷ 12 | (3)
Month 1 | 40 − 24 equipment − 3 | 13
Then | minus 3 a month | 10, 7, 4, 1…
Out of money | during month 6 | (2)
Same plan, same bank balance. Eleven months on the P&L, five in the bank
Nothing was hidden — the loss was ₹42 lakh and the equipment was in the plan; they just do not arrive on the same schedule.   Assumes revenue and cash costs fall evenly across the year, the equipment is paid for in month 1, and nothing else moves.

## Slide 8

AND THE PART NOBODY TELLS YOU
So when do you go and ask for money?
Month 5
You have four weeks of cash. Every investor can see it. You will take whatever terms you are offered, if anyone offers at all.
Month 3
A raise usually takes months rather than weeks — three to six is a working assumption, not a rule. Starting now means closing around the time you run out.
Month 1
Expensive in attention and dilution, and you are negotiating from the only position that works: you do not need it yet.
Runway is not how long you survive. It is how long you can negotiate
Which is why founders talk about months of runway the way a pilot talks about fuel. It is not the whole of your bargaining power — a credible plan, a milestone you have hit and somebody else who wants in all count — but it is the part nobody can argue with.

## Slide 9

32–40 MIN  ·  CASE ONE
When did this company stop losing money?
Eternal | FY21 | FY22 | FY23 | FY24 | FY25 | FY26
Revenue, ₹ cr | 1,994 | 4,192 | 7,079 | 12,114 | 20,243 | 54,364
Operating margin | (23%) | (44%) | (17%) | 0.3% | 3.1% | 2.2%
Net profit, ₹ cr | (816) | (1,222) | (971) | 351 | 527 | 366
Cash from ops, ₹ cr | (1,018) | (693) | (844) | 646 | 308 | 632
Screener, consolidated, 10 October 2026.
The level flipped in FY24
₹43 crore of operating profit on ₹12,114 crore of revenue. A rounding error, and the first positive one.
The direction flipped a year earlier
(44%) to (17%) in FY23. Nothing was profitable yet and the trend had already turned — but a company can improve from a terrible starting point and still not get there.

## Slide 10

Direction turns before level.
By the time a loss-maker reports its first profit, the people who were going to make money have already decided. But direction only tells you where this is heading — the level tells you how far it still has to go, and you need both.

## Slide 11

40–50 MIN  ·  CASE TWO
The loss grew. The business improved.
Swiggy | FY24 | FY25 | FY26
Revenue, ₹ cr | 11,247 | 15,227 | 23,053
Operating margin | (20%) | (18%) | (14%)
Net loss, ₹ cr | (2,350) | (3,117) | (4,154)
Depreciation, ₹ cr | 421 | 612 | 1,217
Interest, ₹ cr | 71 | 101 | 200
Cash from ops, ₹ cr | — | (2,169) | (2,898)
Operating margin improved six percentage points between FY24 and FY26; the absolute net loss grew about 77% over the same two years. Both are in the table — and margin is a share of revenue, which is not the same thing as profit per order.
FACT
what the table shows
HYPOTHESIS
what might explain it
MISSING
what you would need to check
Three minutes, and every sentence you say has to go in one of those three boxes.

## Slide 12

50–54 MIN  ·  THE DISCIPLINE
Three tests for telling the two apart
1
Is the loss shrinking per unit?
Not in rupees — per order, per customer, as a share of revenue. Eternal's margin moved every single year, including the years it stayed negative.
2
Is the loss buying something that stays?
A dark store, a brand, a habit, a network. Or is it buying this month's orders at a discount that ends when the discount ends?
3
Can you see the statements at all?
On time, audited, with a cash flow statement. If you cannot check tests 1 and 2, there is no test 3 — there is only trust.
These do not guarantee anything. They separate a loss with a credible explanation from one you cannot justify on the evidence you have.

## Slide 13

54–74 MIN  ·  INVESTOR PITCH SIMULATION
The board is asked for another ₹3,000 crore
The company is Swiggy. The numbers are the ones on the slide before last.
Left half of the room — the founders
You are asking for the money. Build the case on the three tests. You may use any number on that table and nothing else. You must name what the ₹3,000 crore buys and when it stops being needed.
Right half — the investment committee
You are not against funding loss-makers. You are against funding this one without an answer. Your job is to find the question the founders cannot answer with the table.
Timing | 
3 minutes | On your own, in silence. Your position, and the strongest point against it
12 minutes | Then your half. Four questions you must answer are on the handout
6 minutes | Founders open, committee responds, founders get the last word
2 minutes | Everyone votes — and you may vote against your own side
Six people will speak. Forty will have written. The three silent minutes are what make that true.

## Slide 14

AND WHAT DOES NOT
What wins this argument
Wins | Loses
A number from the table, used correctly | “The market is huge”
Naming what the money buys, specifically | “They have strong brand recall”
A rate of change, not a level | “They are losing ₹4,000 crore, so no”
Saying what would change your mind | Anything that could be said about any company
Conceding a point that is actually true | Not conceding anything
The last row is the one that decides it. An investment committee that cannot be moved is not doing analysis, and a founder who concedes nothing has not read their own numbers. Both sides are being marked on whether they changed their mind about anything.

## Slide 16

SPARE  ·  FIVE MINUTES IF YOU ARE AHEAD
Two more terms, lightly
 | What it is | Example
CAC | What it costs to acquire one customer — all the marketing, divided by the customers it brought | ₹300
Gross profit per customer, per year | What that customer contributes after the cost of serving them | ₹200
How long they stay | The guess | 3 years
LTV | Lifetime value: 200 × 3 | ₹600
LTV ÷ CAC | Investors want roughly 3. This is 2 | 2.0
Three of those four numbers are measured. One is a guess, and it is the one the answer is most sensitive to. Change three years to two and the ratio falls to 1.3 — the business stops working, and not one thing about it has actually changed.

This is a simplified lifetime value: it holds gross profit flat and ignores discounting. And “three” is a rule of thumb, not a standard — 3 does not make a business good, and 2 does not make it unviable.

## Slide 17

80–85 MIN  ·  A NOTE ON HUMILITY
How did the clever valuation age?
Zomato / Eternal | Per share
Damodaran's valuation, July 2021 | ₹41
IPO price band, July 2021 | ₹72 – 76
Market price, 19 October 2026 | ₹325
Live number — check it the morning you teach and change the date with it.
The easy conclusion
He was wrong, so valuation is pointless and you should back the story instead.
The useful one
He published his assumptions. Open the spreadsheet, change the market size, and see which one moved. Nobody who merely liked the story can do that.
The question is not “was he wrong?” It is which assumption would have to change to get from ₹41 to today's price — and whether you could have known that in 2021.
