## Slide 4

CURVEBALL 3
A 10% price cut is never a 10% problem
Stall | Contribution | Falls by | Profit at normal volume | Falls by
A · Chai (60% margin) | ₹9 → ₹7.50 | 17% | ₹900 → ₹600 | 33%
C · Momos (50% margin) | ₹30 → ₹24 | 20% | ₹1,200 → ₹600 | 50%
E · Sandwich (52% margin) | ₹26 → ₹21 | 19% | ₹1,560 → ₹1,010 | 35%
10% off the price. Half the profit.
It hits twice. The discount comes entirely out of contribution, because your costs did not move — that is the first 17 to 20%. Then your fixed costs are unchanged and have to be covered out of less, which is what takes it to a third or a half.

[Notes] COMPUTED. An earlier version of this slide said '10% of the price, 20% of the profit'. That was wrong — 20% is the fall in CONTRIBUTION for momos. Profit falls by half, because fixed costs do not move.

Make them compute contribution first, then profit. The two-stage answer is the lesson: the price cut takes a bite out of contribution, and then operating leverage takes a second bite out of profit.

The general rule for the board: a price cut of x% cuts contribution by x ÷ margin%. At a 25% margin, a 10% cut destroys 40% of contribution — and far more of the profit.

Then ask: whose business can survive a discount war? Straight back to Session 4's quick-commerce question.

[Notes] COMPUTED and the two columns tie. Profit 3,000 − 1,200 − 900 = ₹900. Cash 1,800 − 1,200 − 1,200 − 900 = −₹1,500.

The ₹1,200 credit sale is the office next door — a regular customer with a running tab who settles weekly. Say that in a sentence; it is the most ordinary thing in the world and it is exactly what the field interviews will find (udhaar).

DO NOT REVEAL FAST. Put both columns up and ask: 'the stall made ₹900 today. Where is the ₹900?' Let them hunt for five minutes. They will find ₹1,200 with the office and ₹1,200 in tomorrow's tea leaves.

The breakthrough sentence, when somebody gets there: profit is not a pile of money sitting somewhere.

## Slide 6

Profit tells you what you earned.
Cash tells you what you can pay today.
Both numbers are true. The profit is real — it is just sitting in somebody else's pocket and in a sack of tea leaves.

[Notes] The sentence for the board. Leave it up for the rest of the session; it reappears in Sessions 6, 8 and 9.

You will meet the older aphorism — 'profit is an opinion, cash is a fact'. It is memorable and they will hear it in industry, so it is worth mentioning. But do not lead with it: 'opinion' makes beginners think profit is arbitrary, when it is a measure governed by rules and judgements. The earned-versus-payable version is what they need first.

Thirty days of your money out there. If your supplier gives you 20 days' credit, the gap is 10 — and you only need to fund 10.
Some businesses run this backwards. A supermarket sells your groceries for cash today and pays its suppliers in 45 days — it is funded by its own suppliers, and it never needs a working capital loan.

[Notes] Keep the arithmetic simple: days you hold stock + days customers take to pay − days you take to pay suppliers.

The negative-cycle example is real and worth naming — grocery retail and quick commerce often collect before they pay. VERIFY any specific company figure before quoting one; the general shape is safe, the numbers move.

This is the direct lead-in to Session 9 and to their field interviews, where 'udhaar' is exactly this.

## Slide 9

So why does a profitable business borrow?
What it looks like | What is actually happening
"They must be losing money." | They are profitable. The profit is in receivables and stock.
"They are badly run." | They are growing. Growth consumes cash before it produces any.
"They should just wait." | Suppliers and staff will not wait. Those are paid in cash, now.
A working capital loan can fund the timing gap between paying for the work and collecting for it.
Does borrowing make you more profitable?
No. It costs interest, so slightly less.
Does it make you more liquid?
Yes — and that is the only reason to do it.

[Notes] SOFTENED. An earlier version said a working capital loan 'does not fund losses'. That is too absolute — loss-making companies borrow too, and these facilities also fund stock and other short-term needs. What is true is that the PURPOSE of the facility is the timing gap.

The two cards are the distinction that matters and students almost never make it unprompted: borrowing does nothing for profitability and everything for survival.

## Slide 10

4 MINUTES
Now grow the business
Two businesses, same sales growing 50% a month (₹10,000 → ₹15,000 → ₹22,500 → ₹33,750). Same terms: customers pay a month later, suppliers are paid now, ₹1,000 a month fixed. Only the margin differs — chai 60%, a trader 20%.
Month | Sales | Business 1 (Margin + Fixed Exp) | Business 2 (Margin + Fixed Exp)
1 | ₹10,000 | 60% + ₹1,000 a month fixed | 20% + ₹1,000 a month fixed
2 | ₹15,000 | 60% + ₹1,000 a month fixed | 20% + ₹1,000 a month fixed
3 | ₹22,500 | 60% + ₹1,000 a month fixed | 20% + ₹1,000 a month fixed
4 | ₹33,750 | 60% + ₹1,000 a month fixed | 20% + ₹1,000 a month fixed
Growth eats cash when each extra sale ties up more money than it throws off before the customer pays. At 50% growth and a month of credit, that happens below roughly a one-third margin. The chai stall clears it easily. The trader never does.

[Notes] CORRECTED. An earlier version of this slide showed the chai stall's cash falling every day as volume rose. That was wrong on two counts: it paid for today's ingredients AND tomorrow's stock every day, which buys the stock twice, and it silently assumed credit customers never pay.

Worked honestly, the chai stall FUNDS ITS OWN GROWTH. With a 60% margin, each extra sale throws off far more cash than it ties up, and the stall is cash-positive by month 2.

COMPUTED, monthly: sales grow 50% a month; customers pay one month later; purchases are paid in the month; fixed costs ₹1,000 a month. Chai cost is 40% of sales, the trader's 80%.

The rule for the board: growth burns cash when (cost as a share of sales) × (1 + growth rate) is greater than 1. At 50% monthly growth that means a margin below about 33%. For comparison, the trader at FLAT sales of ₹10,000 a month would generate ₹1,000 of cash a month — it is the growth, not the business, that drains it.

Ask first: would you rather grow 50% a month or 5%? Then show this. The honest answer is 'depends on your margin and how late your customers pay' — which is a far better lesson than 'growth is dangerous'.

Connect back to curveball 3: thin margins are fragile to price cuts AND hungry for cash when growing. Same weakness, two symptoms. And forward to Session 4's quick commerce: thin margins plus fast growth is exactly why that business needs so much capital.

## Slide 12

3 MINUTES  ·  THE APP
Can you make it to the next payment?
The app now shows days of cash on hand. It turns red below two sessions of runway.
What it counts
Cash you can actually spend today. Not your FDs, not your mutual fund units, not what a classmate owes you.
Why that is the right measure
Your suppliers — the class fee, your EMI, a penalty — want cash. They will not take a fixed deposit.
Some of you have been at zero days of runway for three weeks and did not know it had a name.

[Notes] THREE MINUTES, one question, no feature tour. Project the view, ask 'you have this much cash and you owe this much before your next income — are you liquid?', and stop.

Project the runway view. The students at ₹0 cash with money in FDs will see themselves immediately — and this time it is a metric rather than a personal remark.

This is the mechanism the whole session just explained, applied to them. Do not name anyone.

## Slide 15

A film, not a photograph
The income statement
What happened over a period.

A month, a quarter, a year. It measures activity across that stretch of time. The next statement covers the next period.
The balance sheet — Session 8
What you have at one instant.

A photograph, taken on one date. Next year's photograph builds on this one.
"For the year ended 31 March" versus "As at 31 March" — remember those words exactly.
Your stall made ₹900 on Tuesday. That is a P&L. What was in the cash box at closing is a balance sheet.

[Notes] Two minutes. Students conflate these for the rest of the course unless you fix it here. Read the header of a real statement aloud.

This is one of the very few things in the course worth memorising word for word: 'for the year ended' is a film; 'as at' is a photograph.

Avoid saying the P&L 'resets' — some students hear that the business's history disappears. It does not; the new period simply starts counting afresh.
