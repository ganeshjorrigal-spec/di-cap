## Slide 4

THE BASIC P&L LADDER
The ladder
Revenue
What the business earned from selling
− Cost of goods sold
The direct cost of what was sold
= Gross profit
What remains after that direct cost
− Operating expenses
The cost of running the business at all
= EBITDA
Operating profit before depreciation, interest and tax
− Depreciation
Spreading the cost of long-lived assets over the years they earn
− Interest
The cost of borrowed money
− Tax
The state's share
= Net profit
What is left for the owners — earned, not necessarily received
Real statements vary by industry. This is the architecture underneath them.

[Notes] Build this on the board line by line. Do NOT show the finished slide first — let them derive each subtraction.

Each step strips away a different kind of cost, so each subtotal answers a different question: is what we sell worth selling (gross profit), are the operations any good (EBITDA), what is left once everyone has taken their cut (net profit).

On net profit, ask: 'does that mean the owner gets this in their pocket?' Most will say yes. It does not — it is profit, not cash, and the business may keep all of it. That is Session 5's question coming back.

Phrasing notes: revenue is what was EARNED, not what customers were charged — a credit sale is revenue with no cash. Avoid 'EBITDA is before the accountants' — accountants are not a cost line.

WHAT EACH SUBTOTAL IS FOR
Break the ladder open
Gross profit — ₹42,750
Is what you sell worth selling? If this is thin, then fixed expenses needs to be thinner. A price cut lands here first: 10% off would take this stall's EBITDA down 35% and its net profit down 38%.
EBITDA — ₹20,250
Are the operations any good, ignoring how you financed them and what you bought years ago? Two identical shops, with different capital structure, are comparable here and nowhere else.
Net profit — ₹14,000
After the lender, the taxman and the wear on your equipment. It is profit, not cash — whether the owner can take ₹14,000 out of the business is a different question.
Same business. Three different questions. This is why nobody quotes one number.

## Slide 13

BEFORE WE START  ·  IMPORTANT
What you will actually see when you open it
The statutory P&L
What the company must file, under Ind AS.

Costs are grouped by NATURE:
employee benefits expense · finance costs · depreciation and amortisation · other expenses

There is no "gross profit" line. There is no "EBITDA" line.
The analyst's ladder
What we built last week.

Costs grouped by FUNCTION:
what it cost to deliver · what it cost to run the business

So when you open Swiggy's filing and cannot find "gross profit" — nothing is wrong. It was never there.

[Notes] NEW, AND THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT ADDITION TO THIS SESSION. Under Ind AS Schedule III, Indian listed companies present expenses BY NATURE — employee benefits, finance costs, depreciation and amortisation, other expenses. Gross profit and EBITDA are not statutory line items.

Without this slide, forty students open a real filing, cannot find the lines you taught them, and conclude either that they misunderstood or that the company is hiding something. Neither is true.

Say it plainly: the ladder is an analytical overlay, not the legal format. Rebuilding filed numbers into the ladder IS the analyst's job — and it is what they are about to do for the first time.

## Slide 14

THE METHOD  ·  32–37 MINUTES
How to read any P&L in five minutes
1
How big, and what happened?
Revenue, then net profit. Bottom, then top. Everything between is the explanation.
2
What eats the money?
Find the biggest cost line. In a delivery business it is not what most people guess.
3
At which level does profitability disappear?
Gross, operating, or only after interest and tax? Three very different diagnoses.
4
Anything unusual?
'Other income', 'exceptional items', 'share-based payments'. The notes hold the interesting sentences.
Four minutes, not four hours. This is what an analyst does before deciding whether a company deserves a full day.

[Notes] Step 3 is deliberately phrased as 'at which level does profitability disappear' rather than 'find the first negative subtotal'. The three cases are genuinely different diagnoses: negative gross profit is a unit-economics problem; positive gross but negative EBITDA is an operating-cost problem; positive EBITDA but negative net profit is financing, depreciation or tax.

Put this four-step method on the board and leave it. It is reusable, and Session 12 builds on it.

## Slide 18

THE LIMIT OF THE STATEMENT
Can the P&L tell you whether advertising is wasteful?
Discounts, free delivery, cashback. Growth you paid for. Ask the room whether this line is money well spent.
No. It tells you how much was spent.
It cannot tell you what revenue would have been without it. That number does not exist anywhere.
What the statement does
Records what happened. Every rupee, faithfully.
What it does not do
Explain why it happened, or what would have happened otherwise.
A sophisticated idea, hiding inside a beginner course. Say it once and let it sit.

[Notes] Ask the question and let them answer yes first. Most will. Then the correction.

Financial statements record outcomes. They do not contain counterfactuals. Once students have that, a great many bad inferences become impossible — and it is the honest answer to 'this company would be profitable if it stopped discounting. Should it?' Nobody knows, and the P&L will never say.

## Slide 19

68–76 MINUTES
Adjusted EBITDA: what changed?
EBITDA already excludes interest, tax and depreciation. Adjusted EBITDA excludes whatever else management judged unrepresentative.
Commonly adjusted out | The argument for | The argument against
Share-based payments | No cash leaves the company | Real pay, and it dilutes you
One-off restructuring | It will not recur | It recurs every single year
Losses in a new business | The mature business is profitable | You chose to fund the new one
Impairments | A write-down of a past mistake | It was still a mistake
If management chooses what to exclude, what stops this number being meaningless?
Find the reported number first. Then the adjusted one. Then ask what moved, and whether you would add it back every year.

[Notes] TITLE CHANGED from 'the number companies prefer you look at' — punchy, but it teaches automatic cynicism. The habit you want is neither trust nor distrust: understand what you are being shown.

Some adjustments are genuinely informative. A company excluding a new venture's losses may be helping you see the core business.

The question in the dark band is the one that makes this block work. Let them argue. The honest answer is: nothing formally stops it — which is why you always read the reported number alongside it, and why 'would you add this back every year?' is the test that matters.

## Slide 20

76–82 MINUTES
Other income: profit that may not come from the core business
What is usually in it
Interest on cash in the bank. Gains on investments. Foreign exchange. Sale of an asset. Rent from a spare property.
Why it is not automatically bad
A company sitting on a large cash pile earns real interest. For a recently-listed company that is a structural feature of how it was funded, not a failure.
The question is not "is it bad?" It is: what share of the bottom line comes from serving customers, and what share from managing cash?
Three tests: is it recurring, is it material, and is it related to what the company actually does?

[Notes] SOFTENED. An earlier version said 'if a company's profit comes from other income, it is not coming from the business' — too absolute, and it contradicted the very next card on the same slide.

Recurring, material, related-to-core: those three tests do the work. Treasury income on IPO proceeds is legitimate and structural; a one-off gain on selling a building is not the same thing.

Have them do the subtraction on their tracked company anyway — thirty seconds, and it tells them how much of the bottom line is operating.

## Slide 21

82–87 MINUTES
Does the profit turn into cash?
Compare cash generated from operations against reported net profit, over several years.
Roughly equal, most years
The profit is arriving as cash.
Cash consistently lower
Money is going into receivables or stock. Ask why.
Cash far lower, for years
Investigate. Working capital, growth, timing, acquisitions — or something more concerning.
Cash is harder to ignore than accounting profit. If the gap persists, find out why.

[Notes] TWO LINES REMOVED. The earlier version said a persistent gap is 'the pattern that precedes most accounting scandals' and that 'you cannot fake cash for long'. Both are too absolute, and beginners compress them into 'cash below profit means fraud'. A persistent gap far more often means working capital, growth, timing or acquisitions.

What survives is the habit: notice the gap, and go and find out why. The cash flow statement is where you look, and that is Session 9.

They have not met that statement yet. Have them locate the operating cash flow line now without explaining how it is built.
