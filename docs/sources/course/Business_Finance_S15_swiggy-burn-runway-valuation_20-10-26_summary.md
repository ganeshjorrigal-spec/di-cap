Reading Between the Numbers: What Financials Reveal and What They
Hide

The Five Questions That Structure Any Financial Analysis
Before you can read a company's financials usefully, you need a sequence. Not just a checklist
of ratios, but an order of inquiry that moves from the broadest to the most specific. Five
questions do this work:

•      What does the business sell, and to whom? This is the business model. Money flows
       in from customers, money flows out to suppliers and employees. Understanding this first
       means every number you read later has a context.
•      Where does the money come from and where does it go? Revenue sources, major
       expense categories, the general shape of the profit and loss.
•      What is the journey from revenue to net profit? Which costs dilute margin at each
       stage? Where is the business losing the most ground between the top line and the
       bottom?
•      What does the company own, what does it owe, and who has a claim on it? This is
       the balance sheet.
•      How does profit become cash, and how efficiently does that conversion happen?
       This is the cash flow statement.

The first two live in the income statement. The fourth is the balance sheet. The fifth is the cash
flow statement. Understanding all three together is what gives you a complete picture of any
business.

These questions apply universally. What changes is the explanation, not the logic.

A gross margin of 2.2% at a food delivery company and 14% at a consumer goods company
cannot be compared directly. The food delivery business earns on volume and delivery
economics. The consumer goods company earns on brand and distribution. Comparing those
percentages without understanding the business model produces a meaningless ranking. The
framework holds. The numbers only make sense inside the business they came from.


Revenue Is a Reporting Choice, Not Just a Business Outcome
When a company's revenue jumps sharply, the instinct is to conclude the business is growing.
That conclusion is almost always premature.

Revenue can jump for reasons that have nothing to do with underlying business
performance.

The Zomato parent company Eternal is a useful example. Revenue on paper tripled partly
because Blinkit started buying and owning grocery inventory directly rather than acting purely
as a marketplace. That change in inventory ownership changed how revenue is recognised.
Food delivery revenue, the core business, grew 26%. The headline number looked like an
explosion. The actual operational story was more modest.

When you see a revenue jump, the first questions are:

•      Did the accounting policy or revenue recognition approach change?
•      Did a new segment start contributing, or did an existing segment change its model?
•      What does the segment note say? What does the policy note say?

    Revenue is a reporting choice as much as a business outcome.

None of this means revenue growth is fake. It means you need to understand what kind of
growth it is before forming a view.


What the Gap Between Cash and Profit Actually Tells You
A company reports net profit of Rs 100 crore. Cash from operations is Rs 50 crore. There is a
Rs 50 crore gap. What does that gap mean?

The tempting answer is that something is wrong. The right answer is: you do not know yet. The
gap has several possible explanations:

•      Working capital movement. Receivables went up, or payables were paid early. Cash
       is tied up in the operating cycle, not lost.
•      Non-cash expenses. Depreciation reduces net profit without touching cash. A large
       depreciation charge means profit looks lower than the cash actually generated from
       operations.
•      Revenue recognised before cash is received. A builder who collects 100% upfront
       has more cash than revenue in early phases. A company that books revenue before
       collection has the reverse.

The reverse case is equally instructive. If cash from operations is Rs 100 crore and net profit is
Rs 50 crore, you might assume the business is strong. But the right question is: why is cash
higher? Is it because suppliers are being paid late, stretching payables to generate apparent
cash? Or is it genuinely strong collection? Working capital movement is the first thing to check.

Do not read the cash-to-profit gap as a verdict. Read it as a question that working
capital, non-cash items, and timing differences will answer.

A practical example: a builder sells a flat for Rs 100 crore and collects the full amount on day
zero. Construction takes 18 months. The cash is already in the bank. The revenue cannot all be
booked yet. The builder shows a large liability (the obligation to deliver), while the cash figure
looks strong. Profit and cash are deliberately misaligned here, and correctly so.


Other Income: Neither Ignore It Nor Dismiss It
A company with EBITDA of Rs 10 crore, other income of Rs 20 crore, and net profit of Rs 30
crore looks profitable. But the operational engine is only generating Rs 10 crore. The remaining
Rs 20 crore comes from interest income.
The wrong response is to exclude the other income entirely on the grounds that it is not from
core operations. The right response is to ask two questions: Is it recurring? Is it related to how
the business is managed?

    Do not disregard other income just because it is not from operational activity. Ask
    whether it is a recurring phenomenon and what its nature is.

A concrete example makes this precise. A sales head sells a chair to Dmart for Rs 99 instead
of Rs 100, asking for immediate payment rather than payment in 6 months. On paper, the
margin drops from Rs 20 to Rs 19. A less careful analyst flags this as a bad deal. But the Rs 99
collected today goes into a fixed deposit earning 6% annually. Over 6 months, that generates
roughly Rs 3 in interest income. Total cash generated: Rs 102 versus Rs 100 in the original
deal. The version that looked worse at the EBITDA line was better overall.

The key test: is the other income a one-time event or a structural feature of how the
business manages its money?

If Zomato consistently collects cash early and earns interest on the float, that other income is
part of how the business operates. Excluding it gives an incomplete picture. If it is a one-time
asset sale, exclude it. The category alone does not tell you which it is. You have to look.


Margins Falling While Revenue Rises: Mix Shift, Not Collapse
When operating margin falls as revenue grows, the immediate reaction is that the business is
losing cost control. This may be true. It may also be completely wrong.

Two other explanations deserve equal consideration before drawing conclusions:

•      Mix shift. If a new, lower-margin segment is growing faster than the core, blended
       margins will fall even if every individual product line is stable or improving. This is
       arithmetic, not deterioration.
•      Accounting or policy change. A change in how costs are classified can move a
       number significantly without any change in the underlying economics.

The test is to separate the segments and look at each individually before drawing conclusions
about the whole. A company growing its lower-margin quick-commerce business faster than its
higher-margin food delivery business will show margin compression. That does not mean the
food delivery business got worse.

A falling margin on rising revenue is a hypothesis, not a conclusion. Dig into the mix
before forming a view.


What Startup Investing Is Actually Betting On
Investors fund loss-making companies because they are not buying today's income statement.
They are buying an assumption about the future.

The Uber case illustrates this precisely. Damodaran valued Uber at $5.9 billion in 2014. Bill
Gurley valued it at $450 billion. Both were accomplished analysts. Both used sound reasoning.
The difference came entirely from their assumptions about TAM, market share capture, and the
pace of expansion.

Damodaran, the argument goes, was valuing the car market as it existed at the time, like
valuing an automotive company based on the number of horses before the automobile era.
Gurley was valuing the market as it could become.

The TAM assumption is the single biggest driver of disagreement in startup valuations.
Two people who agree on every other input can produce valuations that are orders of
magnitude apart if they disagree on market size.

A startup's business case to investors has a specific structure:

•        Today's revenue is small, but fixed costs do not scale proportionally with revenue. If
         revenue grows 10x, the fixed cost base does not grow 10x. That operating leverage is
         where the future profitability lives.
•        Discounts and below-cost pricing are buying a habit, a network, and a brand. These are
         assets even though they appear nowhere on the balance sheet.
•        Today's margin is not the steady-state margin. It is the margin before discounts stop and
         before fixed costs are spread across a much larger revenue base.

       Cash going out is an asset being built: a network, a habit, a brand.

Zomato changing how often people go downstairs to buy milk is a real economic outcome. The
frequency reduction is measurable. The habit is durable. The investor is pricing that durability,
not the current quarter's numbers.


Burn Rate and Runway: How to Calculate Them Correctly
Two numbers matter more than any others for an early-stage company: burn rate and runway.

Burn rate is net cash out per month. Runway is how long that cash lasts before you run
out.

The most common mistake is calculating burn from the net loss figure on the income statement.
Net loss includes non-cash items like depreciation and does not capture working capital
movements. The right source is the cash flow statement.

A worked example:

•        Annual revenue: Rs 60 lakh (assumed fully cash)
•        Annual cash operating cost: Rs 96 lakh
•        Depreciation: Rs 6 lakh (non-cash, excluded)
•        Net loss on PNL: Rs 42 lakh
•        Equipment purchase in month one: Rs 24 lakh (one-time capex)
•        Cash in bank at month zero: Rs 40 lakh

Cash burn per month: (Rs 96 lakh minus Rs 60 lakh) divided by 12 = Rs 3 lakh per month.
Month one cash position: Rs 40 lakh minus Rs 3 lakh (operating burn) minus Rs 24 lakh
(equipment) = Rs 13 lakh remaining.

From month two onward, Rs 3 lakh leaves each month. Rs 13 lakh divided by Rs 3 lakh = 4.3
months more. Total runway: approximately 5.3 months from day zero.

If you calculate burn using the net loss figure, you include depreciation, which is
non-cash. Your burn number will be wrong, and your runway calculation will be wrong.

Runway is not just survival time. It is negotiating time.

    Runway is essentially how long you can negotiate.

If you know you have 5 months of cash, when do you start raising? Not in month 4. Fundraising
takes time. The later you start, the worse your negotiating position. A founder who starts in
month 1 has options. A founder who starts in month 5 takes whatever is offered.


Direction Turns Before the Level: What Investors See Early
Zomato's operational loss went from Rs 4,400 crore in FY21 to nearly breakeven in FY24. But a
sharp analyst watching the trend in FY23 would have noticed that losses had halved from the
prior year, that cash burn from operations was narrowing as a percentage of revenue, and that
the unit economics per order were improving steadily.

The people who made money on that story did not wait for the first profitable quarter.
They acted on the direction of travel before the destination was reached.

    Direction turns before the level. By the time a loss-maker reports its first profit, the
    people who were going to make money have already decided. The direction tells you
    where this is heading. The level tells you how far it still has to go.

Three tests for assessing whether losses are structural or transitional:

•      Is the loss per unit or per order shrinking? If each transaction is getting closer to
       breakeven, the fixed cost base just needs more volume to cover it.
•      Is the gap between EBITDA and net profit explained by depreciation and interest on
       expansion capex? If so, those costs are investments in future capacity, not recurring
       operational waste.
•      Is operating cash outflow growing slower than revenue? If outflow is growing at 34%
       while revenue is growing at 50%, the business is becoming more cash-efficient even as
       it spends.


The Swiggy Exercise: Putting Founder and Investor Cases Together
Using Swiggy's numbers across FY24 to FY26:

•      Revenue: Rs 11,000 crore, Rs 15,000 crore, Rs 23,000 crore
•      Operating margin (loss): negative 20%, negative 18%, negative 14%
•      Net loss: Rs 2,000 crore, Rs 3,100 crore, Rs 4,000 crore
•      Net margin: negative 18%, roughly negative 21%, negative 17% to 18%

The headline looks alarming: losses in absolute terms nearly doubled. But operating margin
improved by 6 percentage points. Revenue more than doubled. The ratio of loss to revenue
moved only slightly.

The absolute loss number and the loss-as-a-percentage-of-revenue number tell very
different stories. A founder pitches the percentage. An investor worries about the
absolute.

The gap between EBITDA improvement (6 percentage points) and net profit improvement
(roughly 2 percentage points) points to rising depreciation and interest expense. That suggests
expansion: dark stores, equipment, infrastructure. It is a plausible explanation. It is also
something an investor should push back on, because it only works if the expansion generates
the revenue growth needed to justify it.

Key questions an investor should demand answers to before committing:

•      Which single number on the table is evidence that the model is working? (Operating
       margin trend is the strongest case.)
•      Which single number is most worrying? (Net cash outflow from operations is growing in
       absolute terms. Interest expense is rising, suggesting growing debt obligations.)
•      In which year does the company stop needing capital like this? What on the table makes
       that credible?
•      What does the Rs 3,000 crore investment go toward, and what milestone does it buy?

Being against funding loss-makers is not the investor's job. Being against funding
founders who cannot answer the hard questions is.

Generic arguments, on either side, carry no weight. Saying the market is large enough or that
the losses are too big are both positions any person could take about any company without
reading a single number. The useful version is always pinpointed: "Your operating margin
improved by 6 percentage points while revenue doubled. But net profit barely moved 2 points.
What happened between EBITDA and the bottom line, and is that a one-time expansion cost or
a structural feature of your business?"

That is the difference between a question worth asking and noise.


Key Takeaways
1. Any financial analysis follows five questions in order: business model, revenue and cost
flows, the journey from revenue to net profit, the balance sheet, and cash conversion. The logic
is the same for every company; only the explanations differ.

2. A revenue jump does not confirm business growth. Check whether accounting policy,
revenue recognition, or segment mix changed before drawing any conclusion.

3. A gap between net profit and cash from operations is a question, not a verdict. Working
capital movement, non-cash items like depreciation, and timing differences all create that gap
for legitimate reasons.

4. Other income should not be automatically excluded when valuing a business. The right test
is whether it is recurring and whether it reflects how the business actively manages its cash.

5. Falling margins alongside rising revenue may reflect a mix shift or accounting change rather
than operational deterioration. Separate the segments before concluding anything.

6. Investors fund loss-making companies because they are pricing future TAM, operating
leverage, and unit economics improvement, not the current income statement. Two analysts
with different TAM assumptions can produce valuations that are orders of magnitude apart.

7. Burn rate must be calculated from the cash flow statement, not the net loss figure.
Depreciation is non-cash and must be excluded. Runway is how long you can negotiate, not
just how long you can survive, so fundraising should begin well before cash runs out.

8. In startups, direction turns before the level does. The trend in per-unit economics, operating
margin, and cash burn efficiency signals a turning point long before the first profitable quarter
appears.

9. In a founder-investor negotiation, generic arguments carry no weight on either side. The
useful question is always pinpointed to a specific number that either cannot be explained or has
not yet been defended.
